**Quick summary**

- A sociologist of religion writing in The Conversation says [Russell Brand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Brand) was sentenced to ten years and six months in prison for rape and sexual assault in November 2026.
- Reporters noted that Brand used a rosary during sentencing, and a crucifix was seen through the window of the prison van.
- The author argues his public faith fits a trend he calls the "blokeification" of Christianity.
- The piece is analysis and opinion, not a new court report.

The way Russell Brand has displayed his Christianity has become part of the story around his sentencing. According to [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/russell-brand-and-the-blokeification-of-christianity-297580), the comedian was handed ten years and six months in prison for rape and sexual assault in November 2026, and the rosary he used during the hearing is what caught the eye of news outlets.

The article's author, who is both a sociologist of religion and an ordained minister, says they are not trying to judge whether Brand is a sincere believer. Their interest is in what a very public display of faith says about religion, masculinity and celebrity culture.

## The rosary and the prison van

The Conversation says several outlets reported on Brand's use of a rosary while he was sentenced. Reporters also saw a crucifix through the window of the van that carried him away. The author treats these as images that stood out, and uses them as a way into a wider argument rather than as evidence about his private beliefs.

For readers following the case, our earlier coverage of [Russell Brand's family visiting him in prison](https://moduscarry.com/russell-brand-prison-visit/) and the [rise in UK abuse reports after his conviction](https://moduscarry.com/russell-brand-conviction-ciisa-reports/) gives more on the legal and personal fallout.

## What "blokeification" means

The author defines the "blokeification" of Christianity as an effort to reshape faith through a masculine, often laddish lens. It pushes back against the idea that church is too formal, sentimental or feminine. They point to churches where belief is discussed over a pint, where football and banter build community, and where leaders present themselves as ordinary, relatable blokes.

They add that there is nothing wrong in principle with making church more relatable to men. The question they raise is what kind of masculinity is being projected. They say it is especially visible in online Christian circles where faith blends with gym culture, self-improvement and traditional gender roles.

While many religious groups are working against the version of masculinity promoted by figures such as [Andrew Tate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Tate), the author says it is just as important to look at the ideals Christianity itself may be building. They also note that populist and far-right movements borrow Christian symbols, so faith becomes more about cultural identity and strength than belief or practice.

## Victorian roots

The piece traces the idea back to the muscular Christianity of the mid-Victorian era. From the 1850s, some clergymen and educators felt the church had become too passive and domestic. Writers including [Charles Kingsley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Kingsley) and [Thomas Hughes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hughes) pushed to reclaim it through physical strength and moral character.

That link between church and sport left a mark. The author says Manchester City, Aston Villa and Bolton Wanderers all began as church teams in the late 1800s. The Boys' Brigade and the YMCA, founded in London in 1844, followed a similar pattern. The method has barely changed: take spaces where men already gather and fill them with Christian meaning, whether rugby pitches then or pubs, gyms and podcasts now.

## Where Brand fits

The author says Brand's public conversion looked less like a sudden Damascus-road moment and more like a rugged, performative display shaped by celebrity culture. His baptism in the River Thames, in swimming shorts, was led by adventurer [Bear Grylls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bear_Grylls) rather than an ordained minister. The piece says Brand later repeated the ritual for another convert. Responding to the backlash, he said it might seem soon to baptise people, but "the Apostles did it on day one".

The author also points to a Bible carried to court and a seven-day guide to becoming a Christian. They suggest his difficulty recalling scripture in interviews raises questions about the gap between public performance and substance. These are the author's views, not established facts about Brand's beliefs.
