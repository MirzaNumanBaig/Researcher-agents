**Quick summary**

- [Cate Blanchett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cate_Blanchett) was presented with Japan's Praemium Imperiale in Tokyo on Wednesday 28 October, according to W Magazine.
- Princess Hitachi handed over the prize, which Blanchett earned for her work in theatre and film and her humanitarian record.
- Before the ceremony she spoke about doubt as the starting point of her creative work.
- WWD reports she is due to switch on the Champs-Élysées Christmas lights in Paris on 22 November.

The Australian actor has had a busy week on two continents. On Wednesday in Tokyo, Cate Blanchett accepted the Praemium Imperiale from Japan's Princess Hitachi, a prize often described as the arts world's answer to the Nobel. Next month she is expected in Paris, where she is scheduled to light up one of the most famous avenues in the world.

## A career honour from the Japanese Imperial Family

[W Magazine](https://www.wmagazine.com/fashion/cate-blanchett-praemium-imperiale-japan-royalty-suit) reports that the award recognised Blanchett's work in both theatre and film, as well as her long record of humanitarian efforts. The Japan Art Association created the Praemium Imperiale in 1988 to honour achievement in painting, sculpture, architecture, music and theatre or film.

The list of past winners gives a sense of the company she now keeps. It includes the late artist [Yayoi Kusama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yayoi_Kusama), the composer Steve Reich and the director [Ang Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ang_Lee).

Blanchett shared the stage this year with several other laureates. W Magazine names sculptor Andy Goldsworthy, artist [Jenny Holzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jenny_Holzer) and architect [Daniel Libeskind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Libeskind) among them. All appeared together at a press conference the day before the ceremony.

## Why she says doubt matters

The most quoted moment came before the presentation. Blanchett told the audience that, for a woman, doubt is often read as a lack of confidence, and that she sees it differently. "That doubt is my artistic starting point," she said, adding that it lets her hear, see and sense.

She went further, arguing that in a world "riddled with unthinking certainty", doubt is vital to the creative process because it starts the questioning and exploration. It is a short, direct statement of method from an actor whose career has leaned toward difficult, divisive roles.

## The suit that drew the fashion attention

Fashion coverage focused on what she wore. At the ceremony, W Magazine says, she chose a tailored black suit with asymmetrical lapels: one conventional, the other with an oversized, origami-like fold set against a white underlay. A wide black belt finished the look.

The day before, at the press conference, she wore a different black blazer with leather trousers and hot pink aviator glasses. For the photo call afterwards she switched to looser slacks. W Magazine's verdict was that three looks came from a small set of black basics, in keeping with Blanchett's reputation for repeating outfits.

## Next stop: the Champs-Élysées

Blanchett's calendar then moves to Paris. According to [WWD, as carried by Yahoo Finance Singapore](https://sg.finance.yahoo.com/news/champs-lys-es-banks-cate-191432505.html), she is scheduled to switch on the avenue's Christmas lights on 22 November. It will be the first ceremony under new Paris Mayor Emmanuel Grégoire.

The lights are made up of 32 miles of garlands wound across 400 trees. The avenue's business committee is counting on the display to bring back shoppers put off by images of looted shops during protests on 17 and 24 October.

For scale, last year's illuminations drew 14.3 million people between 16 November and 3 January, with a single-day record of 530,000 on 27 December. Annual footfall on the avenue rose 10 per cent in 2025 to 73 million visitors, but only 49 million came in the first nine months of 2026.

A Christmas market returns for the first time in 10 years, with 200 wooden chalets, an ice-skating rink and a merry-go-round. The new light show runs for five minutes every quarter of an hour, set to Max Richter's reworking of Vivaldi's "Four Seasons".
