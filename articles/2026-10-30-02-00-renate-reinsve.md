[Renate Reinsve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renate_Reinsve) has drawn the strongest praise in an otherwise mixed review of *Fjord*, the new Norway-set drama from Romanian director Cristian Mungiu. Writing for The Inlander on 29 October, the critic singles out her performance as the most authentic part of a film they describe as well acted but emotionally remote.

## What Fjord is about

According to The Inlander, the film follows an immigrant Romanian family who move to a remote village in Norway. Before long they become the subject of an all-consuming investigation into possible child abuse. The reviewer describes a story in which people turn on one another and retreat into separate corners, and where a culture war leaves everyone losing something.

Reinsve plays Lisbet, wife of the conservative family patriarch Mihai, played by [Sebastian Stan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastian_Stan). The review says an investigation by child protective services and an eventual trial form the backbone of the plot, without delivering a clear truth or a route to healing for the community.

## The performances

The critic says the two leads bring real life to material that could otherwise feel lifeless. Stan, who is described as actually bald and unrecognisable compared with his Marvel work, gets credit for transforming himself.

Reinsve is the standout. The review highlights her work as Lisbet, who is shown working as a nurse and caring for people who have died. According to The Inlander, she reveals the character through small, quiet movements rather than speech, and leaves the audience curious about what moves her. The critic says viewers understand Lisbet even when she says nothing at all.

## Where the review finds fault

The praise stops at the performances. The reviewer argues that Mungiu holds his characters at such a distance that viewers never bond with them, so the family's fraying never carries weight. The film, they write, becomes a story about people talking past each other that simply repeats the problem, offering beautifully shot surfaces and little more.

The critic also says the children at the centre of the case almost vanish from the film and do not speak about events until the very end, which makes them pawns in the culture war the film seems to examine. They credit it with raising worthwhile questions about the state's power to remove rights from families and a society's duty to its children.

The review compares *Fjord* unfavourably with Mungiu's earlier film *R.M.N.*, set in a Transylvanian village and based on a real event in Ditrau, Romania, where 1,800 villagers voted to expel three Sri Lankan bakery workers. Fjord is also drawn from real cases of the state separating families in Norway, according to the same review.
