**Quick summary**

- Lyricist [Bernie Taupin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernie_Taupin) told the Los Angeles Times that Bob Dylan's 17-minute song Murder Most Foul was "overly hyped".
- He praised the album it sits on, Rough and Rowdy Ways, as "inspirational" and said Dylan is still vibrant.
- Taupin called the track "rambling" and said any good lyricist could have written an equally good song.
- The comments were reported by Far Out Magazine on 24 November 2026.

Bob Dylan Murder Most Foul has drawn praise for years, but [Bob Dylan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dylan) does not win over every fellow songwriter. Bernie Taupin, the lyricist best known for his work with Elton John, has said that the song was "overly hyped", according to [Far Out Magazine](https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/bernie-taupin-believes-bob-dylan-murder-most-foul-overly-hyped-just-rambling/), which quoted a new Los Angeles Times interview.

## What Taupin actually said

Taupin did not open with criticism. He described Dylan's 2020 album Rough and Rowdy Ways as "inspirational" and explained why: Dylan, he said, is "still vibrant" and still writing about interesting things.

Then the tone shifted. "He doesn't always write things that I love," Taupin said, adding that he sometimes finds Dylan sloppy. The one track he did not care for on the record was the Kennedy song, which he felt had been hyped beyond its worth.

Pressed on why, he was blunt. He said he did not think it was very good and that any really good lyricist could have written something equally strong. He also picked at the song's name-dropping, pointing to its references to Henley, Frey and Fleetwood Mac. He said he did not disrespect those acts, but to him the passage was simply rambling.

## The song at the centre of it

Murder Most Foul runs for roughly 17 minutes. Far Out Magazine describes it as a rundown of American cultural history across about 60 years, built around the assassination of John F. Kennedy in 1963. That length and ambition are a large part of why it drew so much attention when it arrived, and why a cooler verdict from a respected songwriter stands out.

Taupin's complaint is about craft rather than about Dylan's standing. He still holds Dylan's wider work in high regard, and he framed the Kennedy track as an exception on an album he otherwise enjoyed.

## A nod to Mick Jagger

Taupin also said he could picture [Mick Jagger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mick_Jagger) delivering a counter version of the song. He added that nobody writes quite like Jagger and quoted the line "I was a butcher cutting up meat" as the kind of image few others could produce.

His admiration for Jagger did not extend to the Rolling Stones' latest album, Foreign Tongues. Taupin said he does not like it as much as the band's previous record. Far Out Magazine also noted that Taupin recently released his first solo album since 1987, The Sea Has No Mercy, on 23 October 2026.

## Dylan has always split opinion

A divided response is nothing new for Dylan. In a separate feature, [MusicRadar](https://www.musicradar.com/artists/how-bob-dylan-stuck-it-to-the-new-york-folk-scene-with-one-song-many-took-it-personally) looks back at the backlash Dylan faced in 1965 when he went electric, a moment recently depicted in the Timothée Chalamet film A Complete Unknown. Historian [Elijah Wald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elijah_Wald) argued that part of the folk scene felt its world was threatened as rock and roll took hold.

MusicRadar traces how that rejection fed into Positively 4th Street, a song Dylan wrote to needle former friends from the Greenwich Village scene. The album Bringing It All Back Home, released in April 1965, opened with [Subterranean Homesick Blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subterranean_Homesick_Blues), which the magazine describes as a mix of Kerouac and [Chuck Berry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Berry). Sixty years on, the argument has moved from folk purists to fellow songwriters, but the pattern is familiar.
