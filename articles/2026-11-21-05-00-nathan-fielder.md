**Quick summary**

- Time Out has reviewed *You Can See Everything*, the documentary in which [Nathan Fielder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Fielder) spends weeks with Elizabeth Holmes and her partner Billy Evans.
- The reviewer describes the film as hypnotic, uncomfortably intimate and open to many readings, and notes it runs to about three hours.
- Fielder co-directed with [Lance Oppenheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lance_Oppenheim). The film is in UK and Ireland cinemas now.
- According to Wikipedia, it holds a 97% Rotten Tomatoes score and has grossed about US$93 million on a US$7 million budget.

## A review that calls it Fielder's biggest challenge yet

Time Out's new review of *You Can See Everything* treats it as the toughest job yet for [Nathan Fielder](https://moduscarry.com/tag/nathan-fielder/), the deadpan Canadian behind *The Rehearsal* and [*Nathan For You*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_for_You). The reviewer calls the documentary "extraordinary" and "uncomfortably intimate", and it is not a short sit: the review describes a three-hour battle of wills.

The subject is Elizabeth Holmes, the Theranos founder convicted of fraud, and her husband Billy Evans. According to Time Out, Fielder, Oppenheim and a small crew effectively moved into the couple's San Diego beach house for several weeks. That was around the time Theranos had collapsed and Holmes faced an 11-year jail sentence.

## What happens inside the beach house

The first half, Time Out says, goes back over what went wrong at Theranos. The company claimed to have built a machine that could run many blood tests at once, a claim that was shown to be fraudulent. The review suggests viewers will get more from it if they know John Carreyrou's book *Bad Blood* or the Apple TV drama *The Dropout*, which the film weaves into its own story. It adds that Fielder does not try to match the book's forensic approach.

Holmes still comes across as persuasive, the reviewer writes. She leans on semantics, arguing she only said the machine could run different tests, not that it would. Fielder answers in his usual style, sitting her down to watch the Abbott and Costello "Who's on First?" routine (the review gives it as "Who's On Third?") to show how meaning can be misread. According to Time Out, it does not land with her.

## Two ways to read the film

The review's central point is that the film supports "a dozen different" interpretations. You can see Holmes as a misunderstood young founder paying heavily for a few mistakes, or as a skilled manipulator who has invited Fielder into a world that still revolves around her.

Time Out also singles out Evans. He is described as loyal to a fault and unable to question anything Holmes says. When she goes to prison midway through the film, the reviewer says he becomes a more malign presence: he tweets aggressively from her account without permission and starts a business venture of his own. Holmes accepts this calmly, which leaves Fielder stupefied.

The review also points to the access. It is rare for a filmmaker to get this close to a couple so widely vilified, it says, and Fielder, whose stunts are unpredictable, seems an unlikely person to be let in. The film asks why he was given the chance. Holmes's answer on camera, "Why would I deceive you?", is called the most obvious lie in the film.

## How critics and audiences have responded

Time Out is not alone. According to [Wikipedia's entry on the film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/You_Can_See_Everything), which lists a September 2026 release, *You Can See Everything* holds 97% on Rotten Tomatoes from 229 reviews and 97 out of 100 on Metacritic from 43 critics. The same entry says PostTrak audiences gave it a 98% positive score.

The commercial figures are unusually strong for a documentary. Wikipedia lists a budget of US$7 million and a worldwide gross of about US$93 million, roughly 13 times the cost. Those are tracker figures rather than studio-confirmed numbers, so treat them as indicative.
