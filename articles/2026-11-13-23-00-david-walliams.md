**Quick summary**

- The Bureau of Investigative Journalism (TBIJ) published a commentary on 13 November arguing that legal threats were used to silence women in the cases of Russell Brand, David Walliams and Mohamed Al-Fayed.
- The latest allegations about Walliams come from a Guardian and ITV News investigation. He denies any wrongdoing.
- The Metropolitan Police says it has not received any complaints against him.
- A new UK law to curb such tactics is being considered in parliament, but TBIJ says it is not certain to pass.

A new commentary from the Bureau of Investigative Journalism says the legal threats and non-disclosure agreements in the [David Walliams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Walliams) case show why UK law needs to change. According to [TBIJ](https://www.thebureauinvestigates.com/stories/2026-11-13/russell-brand-and-david-walliams-silenced-women-with-legal-threats-its-time-these-were-outlawed), published on 13 November, powerful people use expensive lawyers to keep women quiet. Walliams denies any wrongdoing.

It is an opinion piece, so it argues a case rather than reporting new findings. What it describes is drawn from earlier reporting by other outlets.

## What TBIJ says happened in the Walliams case

TBIJ points to an investigation by the Guardian and ITV News into Walliams, which reported allegations that he groomed young women and girls. Walliams denies the claims, and TBIJ notes the Metropolitan Police says it has not received any complaints against him.

One detail in that reporting is central to the commentary. A woman who was invited to Walliams' house at 18 reportedly received a letter a few months later from the law firm Schillings. It warned that she risked committing a "serious civil offence" if she disclosed private information about him. Schillings told the outlets it had been instructed to write the letter and understood the woman intended to sell an "untruthful" story to the press, according to TBIJ.

TBIJ also cites comedian Lucy Beaumont, who told Sky News that another young woman she spoke to had signed an NDA. That woman had been invited into Walliams' dressing room and was distressed by the experience. The NDA reportedly barred her from discussing it with friends, family or even a therapist.

## Why TBIJ links it to Russell Brand and Al-Fayed

The commentary places Walliams alongside [Russell Brand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Brand) and the late Mohamed Al-Fayed. TBIJ says Brand was sentenced this week to 10 years in prison for raping one woman and sexually assaulting three others. It adds that a woman identified as "Alice" in a Sunday Times investigation said she received a letter from his lawyers in 2020 accusing her of being after money. Her allegations were not among those heard by the court.

On Al-Fayed, TBIJ says it revealed two years ago that the Mail on Sunday was preparing a story in 2008 about an investigation into him over the alleged sexual assault of a 15-year-old. After threats from his lawyers, the paper removed his name from the piece.

TBIJ also cites the costs facing newsrooms. After the Guardian reported allegations about [Noel Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noel_Clarke) and he sued for libel, defending the case, which the paper won, cost about £6m. In October, according to TBIJ, Clarke pleaded not guilty to allegations of sexual assault and other offences against five women.

## The case for changing the law

TBIJ argues the problem is structural. It says UK law favours wealthy people who can pay for lawyers, and that defending a libel claim can cost hundreds of thousands of pounds, if not millions, according to Rupert Cowper-Coles, head of media at the law firm RPC. He told TBIJ that most victims stay silent as a result. RPC advises the Bureau.

Baroness Kennedy, a leading UK lawyer, told TBIJ that the legal system meant to protect women is being "turned against them by wealthy perpetrators, through NDAs and threats of litigation".

The commentary also questions whether the lawyers' conduct in the Walliams letter crossed the line into "heavy-handed tactics that are oppressive or abusive", the test used by the [Solicitors Regulation Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solicitors_Regulation_Authority). The regulator can investigate that, TBIJ says, but critics call it "toothless" after a run of legal defeats. Its conclusion is that only a change in the law will protect women in these situations.

## What happens next

A new law to crack down on these tactics is under consideration in parliament, according to TBIJ, though it may not pass. No finding has been made against Walliams, and he continues to deny the allegations.
