Lawyer Jahan Kalantar says the climate around sexual assault cases helps explain the Russell Brand sentence. He spoke to WHO after Brand was sentenced to 10.5 years in prison following convictions last week.

## What Russell Brand was convicted of

According to [WHO](https://www.who.com.au/entertainment/russell-brand-prison/), Brand was convicted of raping one woman and sexually assaulting three others. The sentence handed down was more than a decade in prison, specifically 10.5 years.

Brand has not accepted the outcome. WHO reports that he continues to deny the charges and maintains that his encounters were consensual. That position was repeated in a statement read by his wife, Laura Brand, on their YouTube channel. Our earlier coverage looks at [what Brand said in his prison message](https://moduscarry.com/russell-brand-prison-message/) and at [what the judge said about consent](https://moduscarry.com/russell-brand-verdict-consent/).

## Why a lawyer says the timing matters

The WHO piece is a commentary, not a court report. Its argument is that the sentence could have been much lighter if it had been handed down at another time. Many people feel 10.5 years is not enough, the article notes, but it also points out that crimes of this kind are exceptionally hard to prove in court.

Kalantar, speaking to WHO, said judges are very good at keeping public noise out of the courtroom and deciding matters of law. He added that judges are still human, and that the mood of the times plays a part.

"I think we need to accept that the zeitgeist does play a role," he said.

## How public conversation can move the range

WHO points to the #MeToo movement, the Cornell case and Jeffrey Epstein as examples of conversations that have kept sexual assault in the public eye. The more prominent the subject, the article argues, the more the range of what is seen as an acceptable sentence can shift.

Kalantar explained that a sentence outside the accepted range can be challenged on appeal, whether it is judged too severe or too lenient. He said different judicial officers look at cases from different perspectives, and that "the law is psychological and not simply logical". In his view, current social issues will be at the front of a judge's mind because of their exposure to them.

His closing advice, as quoted by WHO, was blunt: people who do bad things should stop, and people should check their privilege and entitlement because there will be consequences. The article's own conclusion is that the public noise, however draining, is "moving the needle".

## What this does and does not show

The article reflects one lawyer's view and the publication's own stance. It does not report on any appeal, and it does not say whether Brand will challenge the verdict or the length of his sentence. Readers should treat Kalantar's comments as general observations about sentencing, not as a ruling on this case.
