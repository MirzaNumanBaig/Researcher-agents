**Quick summary**

- [Seth Rogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seth_Rogen) stars in Sephora's holiday campaign "We All Sephora", which launched on 29 October after the retailer announced his casting on 26 October.
- The ad was made by agency GUT New York and directed by [Jason Woliner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason_Woliner).
- It shows Rogen baffled by the range on offer while he shops for gifts, and it has drawn criticism over how it portrays men and beauty.
- Industry site BeautyMatter reports that critics question whether the joke undercuts Sephora's own efforts to include men.

The Seth Rogen Sephora campaign has become one of the more talked-about holiday ads of the season, and not only for its laughs. According to trade publication [BeautyMatter](https://beautymatter.com/articles/seth-rogen-is-confused-at-sephora-so-is-the-internet), the actor and comedian's turn as a confused shopper has prompted a debate about how beauty brands speak to men.

## What the ad shows

Sephora announced on 26 October that Rogen would front its holiday push, and the campaign, titled "We All Sephora", went live on 29 October. GUT New York created it and Woliner directed it, BeautyMatter reports.

The premise is simple. Rogen starts his holiday shopping in a Sephora store and is overwhelmed by the sheer number of products and what each one is meant to do. With help from a sales assistant, he works through the shelves and leaves confident in his choices.

His basket, as described by BeautyMatter, includes an LED mask for his mother, a Dae styling spray for his producer and a vitamin C set for his sister. Along the way he also learns what a setting spray does.

## Why it has split opinion

BeautyMatter says the campaign has proved controversial. The central gag, a man puzzled by beauty products, is the point of contention. Critics quoted in the report ask whether it leans on an outdated stereotype of the clueless male shopper, and whether that sits awkwardly with Sephora's existing work to welcome men into its stores.

The publication sets the debate against the market backdrop. It cites figures showing North America holds the largest share, 34%, of a global men's beauty market valued at $48.86 billion. It also reports that 36% of men believe looking their best matters more than it did five years ago.

On that reading, the joke arrives at an awkward moment. BeautyMatter argues the term "metrosexual" has faded because caring about appearance is now commonplace for many men.

## The case for the campaign

The report also points to the other side of the argument. The campaign, it says, puts accessible holiday gifting ahead of any attempt to redefine how men buy beauty. Seen that way, Rogen is a stand-in for any gift buyer who does not know the category, not a statement about men who use skincare themselves.

Notably, the gifts he buys are all for other people, which fits that reading. The ad is about finding the right present, with the confusion serving the comedy.

## What happens next

The sources do not say how long the campaign will run or whether further spots are planned. Neither Sephora nor Rogen is quoted in the BeautyMatter report on the criticism, so any response from either side remains unconfirmed. The campaign sits in the busiest shopping stretch of the retail calendar, so the reaction will probably be tested in sales rather than in comment threads.
