**Quick summary**

- [Bob Dylan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dylan) and [Tom Petty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Petty) wrote "Tweeter and the Monkey Man" for the 1988 album Traveling Wilburys Vol 1 by trading lines back and forth.
- Petty told Uncut they recorded the exchanges on cassette, then picked the best lines, according to Far Out Magazine.
- The lyrics reference six Bruce Springsteen song titles, though Petty said in 2013 it was meant as praise, not mockery.
- Dylan says in his memoir Chronicles that he was in a deep creative slump before the Wilburys project.
- Separately, 20 November marks 65 years since Dylan began recording his debut album.

Bob Dylan and Tom Petty turned a game of lyrical one-upmanship into one of the stranger songs in the Traveling Wilburys catalogue, according to a [Far Out Magazine](https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/bob-dylan-tom-petty-lyrics-competition-traveling-wilburys-1988/) feature published on 20 November 2026. The song, "Tweeter and the Monkey Man", appeared on the supergroup's 1988 debut and is credited to Dylan, even though it grew out of a back-and-forth with Petty.

## How the song was written

The Wilburys were an unusual lineup: [Roy Orbison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Orbison), Dylan, [George Harrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Harrison), [Jeff Lynne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeff_Lynne) and Petty. Far Out notes how young they were at the time of the recording: Orbison was 52, Dylan 47, Harrison 45, Lynne 40 and Petty 37.

Dylan wrote or co-wrote four of the album's ten tracks, the outlet reports. "Tweeter and the Monkey Man" was the one that drew the biggest reaction. Petty described the process to Uncut: the two would volley lines at each other, record everything to cassette, then go back through the tape and write down the best of it.

Petty told Rolling Stone in 2013 that the idea began with Dylan wanting a song about a character called Tweeter who needed a counterpart. Petty offered "The Monkey Man", and Dylan liked it. Dylan then wanted to set the story in New Jersey.

## The Bruce Springsteen question

The finished lyrics follow a former boy scout who went to Vietnam and later flees across the Jersey line in a stolen car. Far Out counts references to six [Bruce Springsteen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Springsteen) song titles: "Stolen Car", "Mansion on the Hill", "Thunder Road", "State Trooper", "Factory" and "The River". The New Jersey setting and the themes of escape and doomed romance add to the resemblance.

That raised an obvious question about whether the pair were poking fun at Springsteen. Petty rejected that reading in his 2013 Rolling Stone interview. He said Dylan suggested using Springsteen titles and "clearly meant it as praise", adding that nobody was trying to knock anyone. Far Out points out that the references are far more extensive than Petty implied, but says most listeners have chosen to hear the song as affectionate teasing between legends. Dylan himself, the outlet notes, has rarely given a direct answer.

## Why the Wilburys mattered to Dylan

The collaboration came at a low point. Far Out says Dylan toured the world with Petty and the Heartbreakers in 1986 and 1987, and felt it was a career low. He thought for half of that tour that it might be his last as a live performer.

In his 2004 memoir Chronicles, Dylan wrote that Petty was at the top of his game while he was at the bottom of his. He said his own songs had become strangers to him. According to Far Out, things turned quickly afterwards. Working alongside a hero in Orbison, a Beatle in Harrison, a studio specialist in Lynne and an admirer in Petty helped push him forward. His 1989 album [Oh Mercy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oh_Mercy) was widely seen as a return to form, and the Far Out piece says "Margarita" from the Wilburys album hinted at what was coming.

## An anniversary on the same day

The timing is a coincidence, but a neat one. A [RiverBender.com](https://www.riverbender.com/news/details/this-day-in-music-history-nov-20-from-bob-dylan-to-adele-98509.cfm) music history roundup notes that on 20 November 1961, a 20-year-old Dylan walked into Columbia's Studio A in New York to start his first album with producer John Hammond. The self-titled record has 13 tracks, mostly folk, blues and gospel covers such as "Man of Constant Sorrow" and "House of the Risin' Sun", plus two originals, "Talkin' New York" and "Song to Woody". Sessions continued on 22 November, and Columbia released the album on 19 March 1962.

Set beside the Wilburys story, it shows how far his approach to writing and collaborating has travelled. At 20 he was an interpreter of older songs. By 47 he was swapping lines with a younger peer to find his way back to writing. [MusicRadar](https://www.musicradar.com/artists/we-didnt-think-the-nastiness-was-called-for-when-bob-dylan-dropped-a-diss-track-and-burned-his-bridges-with-the-greenwich-village-folk-scene) adds another side of that restlessness in a separate feature, describing how Dylan's move to electric music in the mid-1960s, and the song "Positively 4th Street", cost him friendships in the Greenwich Village folk scene.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who wrote "Tweeter and the Monkey Man"?

The song is credited to Bob Dylan, but Tom Petty said it emerged from the two of them trading lines, which they recorded on cassette and then edited down.

### Is "Tweeter and the Monkey Man" about Bruce Springsteen?

It references six Springsteen song titles and has a New Jersey setting. Petty told Rolling Stone in 2013 that it was not meant to mock Springsteen and that Dylan intended it as praise.

### How old were the Traveling Wilburys when they recorded their debut?

According to Far Out Magazine, Roy Orbison was 52, Bob Dylan 47, George Harrison 45, Jeff Lynne 40 and Tom Petty 37.
