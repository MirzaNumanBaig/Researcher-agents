**Quick summary**

- [Nitesh Tiwari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitesh_Tiwari) graduated from IIT Bombay in 1996 with a BTech in Metallurgical Engineering, according to India Today.
- His creative start came in Hostel No. 4, where he performed mime, wrote skits and directed a musical that won the college's Performing Arts Festival shield.
- He spent 18 years in advertising and became Chief Creative Officer at Leo Burnett, Mumbai, before directing films.
- His two-part Ramayana, reported to carry a budget above Rs 4,000 crore, opened on November 5, and Outlook India says Part 1 gives its supporting cast an unusually large role.

The director now steering one of Indian cinema's biggest projects began as a student who wrote ghost-and-haunted-house skits for hostel competitions. A profile published by India Today on November 13 traces how [Nitesh Tiwari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitesh_Tiwari) went from IIT Bombay's Hostel No. 4 to Ramayana, the two-part epic that reached cinemas on November 5.

## How Nitesh Tiwari's IIT Bombay years shaped him

India Today reports that Tiwari arrived at the campus expecting the usual engineering routine and found something different. In a 2019 interview with Mumbai Mirror, quoted in the profile, he recalled that his seniors noticed his talent for mime and pushed him towards inter-collegiate festivals, including the cultural festival Mood Indigo.

By his second and third years he was writing stories for skits. In his final year he directed A Touch of Evil, a musical about two sorcerers and a pair of lovers caught between them. It won the Performing Arts Festival shield, the contest in which the institute's hostels compete against one another.

The stage was not his only arena. He captained the hostel cricket team, played football, volleyball and hockey, and ran the 4x100-metre relay. India Today says he was named Sportsman of the Year and graduated in 1996 with the institute's Scroll of Honour, a distinction he described as not having been awarded for 17 years.

## From advertising to the director's chair

Tiwari did not go straight into film. After a short spell at a software company he moved into advertising, where, according to IIT Bombay's alumni profile cited by India Today, he spent 18 years at FCB Ulka, Lowe Lintas and Leo Burnett. He rose to Chief Creative Officer at Leo Burnett in Mumbai.

His directing debut came in 2011 with [Chillar Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chillar_Party), which won the National Award for Best Children's Film. [Bhoothnath Returns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhoothnath_Returns), Dangal and Chhichhore followed. India Today also credits him with contributing to the writing of [Nil Battey Sannata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nil_Battey_Sannata) and [Bareilly Ki Barfi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bareilly_Ki_Barfi).

## Ramayana and its supporting cast

The scale of the current project sits a long way from a hostel stage. India Today describes Ramayana as a two-part undertaking with a reported overall budget of more than Rs 4,000 crore. The figure is a reported one, not a number confirmed by the filmmakers in the sources we read.

Outlook India's analysis of Part 1 says the film is led by [Ranbir Kapoor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranbir_Kapoor), Yash and [Sai Pallavi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sai_Pallavi). It argues that the supporting players matter as much as the leads. By opening in Lanka rather than Ayodhya, the outlet says, Tiwari puts figures such as Ravana's mother Kaikesi, played by Shobana, at the centre of the conflict.

Two performances draw the most attention in that review. [Ravie Dubey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravi_Dubey), making his big-screen Bollywood debut as Lakshmana, is described as grounded and witty, a contrast to Kapoor's deliberately restrained Rama. Outlook India adds that veteran actor Sunil Lahri, who played Lakshmana in Ramanand Sagar's 1987 television series, praised the performance, as did Karan Johar. [Rakul Preet Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rakul_Preet_Singh) plays Shurpanakha, whom the film gives a fuller backstory before her confrontation at Panchavati.

The review is not all praise. It says the rush to reach the war in Part 2 leaves Lara Dutta, Sheeba Chaddha and Kajal Aggarwal with trimmed arcs, and that the intimate character moments do not always land.
