**Quick summary**

- [Bob Dylan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dylan) wrote "Clothes Line Saga" in 1967 as a reply to Bobbie Gentry's chart-topping "Ode to Billie Joe", according to American Songwriter.
- The song's working title was "Answer to Ode".
- It was recorded during the Basement Tapes sessions with members of The Band.
- Dylan has never spoken publicly about his intentions, so the motive remains unconfirmed.

Bob Dylan's "Clothes Line Saga", one of the strangest songs from his Basement Tapes period, started out as a response to one of 1967's biggest hits, [Bobbie Gentry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobbie_Gentry)'s "Ode to Billie Joe", according to a retrospective from [American Songwriter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Songwriter).

## What is the story behind Bob Dylan's Clothes Line Saga?

The magazine points to a telling detail: the song's working title was "Answer to Ode". That label links it directly to Gentry's single, which American Songwriter describes as one of the most successful debut singles in pop history. The Mississippi-born singer-songwriter wrote and recorded it, and it reached No. 1 on release in 1967.

Both songs centre on a child, a "Mama" and a "Papa", and both are built from everyday conversations about chores. That shared setup is what the writer sees as Dylan's nod to Gentry's storytelling.

## Why Gentry's song caught Dylan's ear

Fans were drawn to Gentry's sultry delivery and to the central mystery of her song. She never said what was thrown off the Tallahatchie Bridge shortly before the death of the title character, and listeners have wondered ever since. Critics also praised the authentic picture she painted of a small Southern community, including its natural-sounding dialogue.

American Songwriter argues that Dylan saw something in those kitchen-table exchanges that was ripe for borrowing.

## A deliberately low-stakes reply

Where Gentry's song hints at scandal, Dylan's shrugs. According to the article, there are no shocking town secrets or unsolved mysteries in "Clothes Line Saga". Its biggest revelation, that "The Vice President's gone mad", is treated with the same indifference as everything else. Even the word "Saga" promises a dramatic arc the lyrics never try to deliver.

The writer calls it one of the most subtly humorous sets of lyrics in Dylan's vast catalogue. Whether it was meant as satire or affectionate mockery is left open, because Dylan has never discussed it publicly.

## The Basement Tapes backdrop

The song comes from the [Basement Tapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Basement_Tapes) sessions, recorded by Dylan and members of The Band in a pink house in upstate New York. American Songwriter notes that nobody involved knew what would become of the recordings. There was talk of using them as publishing demos, and some artists did cover the material.

Nobody expected the tapes to light up the bootleg market, as curious collectors snapped them up. The writer suggests the musicians might have played it safer had they known, and adds that the weirdness is part of what makes the music wonderful.
