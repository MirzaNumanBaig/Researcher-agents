**Quick summary**

- The NSW Supreme Court dismissed almost every claim in an $11.9 million case against [Rebel Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebel_Wilson) over the film The Deb, according to Australasian Lawyer.
- The judgment, AI Film Production Limited v Wilson [2026] NSWSC 1200, was handed down on 23 October 2026.
- The court found Wilson and her company Camp Sugar committed technical contract breaches, but awarded only $10 in damages.
- The film's production subsidiary was ordered to pay Camp Sugar the $359,000 balance of its producer fee, plus interest, and the companies must pay the defendants' costs.

Rebel Wilson has won a major round in her long-running dispute with the financiers of her directorial debut. The NSW Supreme Court rejected an $11.9 million claim against her on 23 October 2026, finding the companies behind The Deb had not proven that her social media posts caused them any loss, [Australasian Lawyer reported](https://www.thelawyermag.com/au/practice-areas/tmt-telecoms-media-technology/nsw-supreme-court-rejects-production-companys-119m-claim-against-rebel-wilson/595229).

## Who was suing whom

The case pitted UK-incorporated AI Film and related companies against Wilson, who co-produced, directed and starred in The Deb, and her company Camp Sugar. According to the report, AI Film financed the film and co-owned it with Camp Sugar through a joint venture company, on whose board Wilson sat.

The companies alleged that Wilson's Instagram posts criticising the co-producers, along with anonymous websites they said she was behind, left the film unprofitable. AI Film sought $11,861,507.04, the amount it said it had spent.

## Why the main claims failed

The misleading or deceptive conduct claim relied on the Australian Consumer Law, which covers only statements made in trade or commerce. The court found that posts on the film's promotional Instagram account qualified, but posts on Wilson's personal account did not. It noted that the cases cited by the companies involved rival businesses, "not co-producers working on the same project", and found most of the qualifying statements were not misleading or deceptive.

On the anonymous websites, the court found Wilson had them created and that almost all their claims about one co-producer were false and malicious. It also found malice in an Instagram story suggesting the co-producers had stolen money from the film, which the court said they had not done.

Even so, the injurious falsehood claim failed. That claim requires proof of actual damage, and the court noted the websites did not mention the companies. Harm to individuals, it held, did not prove damage to the businesses.

## The numbers did not hold up

The damages case also ran into trouble. The report says the companies did not call the expert evidence they had foreshadowed. They relied instead on a 2022 revenue forecast built on a $15 million budget, when the final budget reached $21.8 million. The court found that forecast unreliable and described AI Film's attempt to claim one global loss across many alleged contract breaches as "fundamentally misconceived."

The directors' duties claim fell too. The court found no evidence that Wilson used her board seat to pressure the companies into selling her the film cheaply.

## A $10 finding and a costs order

The court did find that Wilson and Camp Sugar committed technical breaches of confidentiality, publicity and professionalism clauses. The joint venture's production subsidiary sought only nominal damages, conceding it could not prove loss. The court awarded $10 and said the claim "ought not to have been brought."

On Wilson's side, a cross-claim succeeded. The court ordered the subsidiary to pay Camp Sugar the $359,000 balance of its producer fee, plus interest. It rejected the argument that payment could wait until AI Film recouped its outlay, finding that the risk rested with AI Film. The companies were also ordered to pay the defendants' costs.

## What happens next

The report does not say whether AI Film will appeal, and no statement from either side was included. Any appeal would go to the NSW Court of Appeal.
