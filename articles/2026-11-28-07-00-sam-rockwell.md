**Quick summary**

- [Martin McDonagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_McDonagh) ends [Wild Horse Nine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_Horse_Nine) with the Rolling Stones' "Out of Time" over about five minutes of credits.
- The song follows the final scene between John Malkovich's dying CIA agent and his partner Lee, played by [Sam Rockwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Rockwell).
- There is no mid-credits or post-credits scene, according to Startefacts.
- Critic [Justin Chang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Chang) of The New Yorker found the cue too obvious.
- Spoilers for the ending of the film follow.

The last thing audiences hear in [Sam Rockwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Rockwell)'s new film is a jaunty pop song about being left behind. [Martin McDonagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_McDonagh) runs the Rolling Stones' "Out of Time" through the whole of the *Wild Horse Nine* end credits, and, according to [Startefacts](https://startefacts.com/news/wild-horse-nine-end-credits-song-rolling-stones), the choice is a deliberate joke at the expense of the characters we have just watched say goodbye.

## What happens before the credits

Startefacts reports that Chris, the CIA agent played by John Malkovich, has terminal cancer. He spends his final minutes on a hillside with his partner, Lee, the role Rockwell plays. Chris has asked Lee to end his life when he gives the word. Before that, the two talk about a trip to Venice they never took and sing a little [Hank Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hank_Williams), including "I'm So Lonesome I Could Cry". Then Chris says "Arkansas", the agreed signal.

The film has no mid-credits or post-credits scene. The credits run roughly five minutes, and the Stones song plays from start to finish.

## Why "Out of Time" works

The track first appeared on the British edition of the album *Aftermath* in 1966. Heard on its own, it is a breakup song: a woman stays away too long, returns expecting to pick things up, and finds the singer has moved on. Startefacts argues that [Mick Jagger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mick_Jagger) sounds delighted to be delivering the bad news.

Placed after a mercy killing, that glee becomes the point. The title also does the work of a subtitle, telling the audience that time has run out for Chris. The outlet's reading is that the cheerfulness of the singer sits against the heaviest act Lee has had to carry out.

## Not everyone is convinced

Justin Chang, writing in The New Yorker, found the Stones cue painfully obvious, according to Startefacts, which concedes he has a point. Its own view is that the bluntness is part of the charm, because the song keeps going well after the joke in the title has landed.

The same piece notes that Chris spends much of the film dictating his memoirs. [Carter Burwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carter_Burwell) scores those recollections of political violence with celesta and tuned percussion, which gives a CIA man's account of his working life something close to a lullaby. That gentleness makes the brash closing song more of a jolt.
