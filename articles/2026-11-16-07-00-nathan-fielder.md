**Quick summary**

- The Atlantic has published an essay arguing that the criticism of paying subjects in *You Can See Everything* is more complicated than it looks.
- The documentary was created by [Nathan Fielder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Fielder) and [Lance Oppenheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lance_Oppenheim) and follows Elizabeth Holmes in the days before she reported to federal prison.
- Holmes's partner, Billy Evans, negotiated payment for giving the crew access, a point the film itself stages and questions.
- A closing title card says Evans gave up his profit share before release, according to The Atlantic.

[Nathan Fielder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Fielder)'s documentary *You Can See Everything* is now the subject of a fresh argument about who should be paid when a film is made about a person in the news. In an essay published on 15 November, [The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/11/holmes-documentary-journalistic-ethics/689350/) says the outrage over money changing hands has received less scrutiny than the film deserves.

## What the film does with the money

According to The Atlantic, the documentary, made with director [Lance Oppenheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lance_Oppenheim), is about Elizabeth Holmes, the former Theranos chief executive convicted of fraud, and the days before she reports to prison. The essay says her partner Billy Evans, the father of her two children, comes off worse than she does. Its reason is not a crime but his wish to be paid for taking part.

The film restages a negotiation between Evans, Fielder, Oppenheim and A24 executives. Evans is allowing a crew into his home and letting them record his children, and, as the essay puts it, even in the shower. The executives react with shock, but Evans insists and they agree reluctantly. The essay says the second half of the film then probes the financial motive of both Holmes and Evans. A closing title card reveals that Evans gave up his profit share before the film came out.

## The criticism, and the essay's reply

The Atlantic notes that Reason called the arrangement a "crooked bargain", and that the Los Angeles Times told readers that paying documentary subjects runs against traditional journalistic standards. The essay accepts the core worry: a pay-to-play arrangement can warp the incentives of everyone involved in a project meant to find the truth.

Its argument is that the picture is murkier. The author, Amanda Knox, writes from her own experience. She says she spent more than seven years beating a murder charge in Italy, and that she has no idea how many unauthorised photographs of her exist. A search of her name on Getty Images returns nearly 5,000 results, she says, and if she wants one she has to pay like anyone else. Her conclusion is that journalistic integrity standards can ensure that, in public-interest cases, money flows to everyone except the person at the centre of the story.

She gives her own examples. For *Mouth of the Wolf: Amanda Knox Returns to Italy*, a Hulu documentary directed by her husband, Christopher Robinson, the production paid Reuters US$408 to license a single photograph. The Atlantic paid US$75 to run it. Knox says she was not paid for the hours of interviews and archive access she gave to a 2016 Netflix documentary about her case, despite many people assuming otherwise.

## How the film has fared

The film has been a success by the numbers. Figures on its Wikipedia page, which draws on trade reporting, put the budget at about US$7 million and the worldwide box office at about US$86.5 million, roughly 12 times its cost. It holds 96% on Rotten Tomatoes from 226 reviews and 97 out of 100 on Metacritic from 42 critics, according to the same page. The New York Times critic Manohla Dargis wrote that few movies speak as uncannily to the confusions of the present moment.

Fielder is credited as a co-director and also appears in the cast, alongside Holmes and Christopher Abbott. Our own coverage includes [a critic's take on the film](https://moduscarry.com/nathan-fielder-you-can-see-everything-review/) and [Billy Evans on being cast as the villain](https://moduscarry.com/nathan-fielder-documentary-billy-evans/).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is You Can See Everything about?
It is a documentary by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim about Elizabeth Holmes in the days before she reports to federal prison after her fraud conviction.

### Was Billy Evans paid for the documentary?
The film shows a negotiation in which Evans pushes for payment. A title card at the end says he gave up his profit share before release, according to The Atlantic.

### Is it normal to pay documentary subjects?
The Atlantic cites the Los Angeles Times as saying it is unusual under traditional journalistic standards. The essay argues the issue is more nuanced.
