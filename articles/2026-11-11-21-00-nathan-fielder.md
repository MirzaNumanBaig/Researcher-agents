**Quick summary**

- [Nathan Fielder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Fielder) has made a three-hour film, *You Can See Everything*, about Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes.
- He co-directed it with [Lance Oppenheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lance_Oppenheim), according to JournalArta.
- The first part covers the 34 days before Holmes entered prison; the second uses an actor to recreate their conversations while she serves her sentence.
- It is the first time Holmes has spoken to the press since 2016, JournalArta reports.

Nathan Fielder's new three-hour film, *You Can See Everything*, follows Elizabeth Holmes through the 34 days before she went to prison and then rebuilds their conversations with an actor once she is behind bars, [JournalArta reported on 11 November](https://journalarta.com/2026/11/11/you-can-see-everything-nathan-fielder-elizabeth-holmes/). The result is a documentary that openly asks how much of what it shows can be trusted.

## How Nathan Fielder's You Can See Everything began

Fielder co-directed the project with Lance Oppenheim. According to JournalArta, the idea did not start with him. Holmes and her partner, Billy Evans, pitched a documentary to the production company A24, and Fielder joined later.

The film opens at the rented beach house in Southern California where Holmes and Evans were living. Cameras sit in the bedroom, bathroom and kitchen, which gives the impression of almost total access to their private life. The title plays on that promise, and the film does not let the viewer settle on whether it is kept.

## Part one: 34 days before prison

The first half follows Holmes in her final 34 days of freedom. JournalArta says it shows both the public image of the Theranos founder and a more domestic side, including scenes with her newborn, Ivy.

Holmes was 19 when she founded Theranos, a company that promised a portable machine able to run hundreds of tests from a finger-prick of blood. It did not deliver on that promise. In 2022 she was convicted of three counts of wire fraud and one count of conspiracy, and was sentenced to 11 years in prison. JournalArta notes that she maintains her innocence.

On camera, she tells Fielder, "I'm always being real." Pressed on what she claimed for Theranos, she answers that she "was just trying to communicate the best I could." Fielder's task, as the report describes it, is to weigh that account against the conflicting versions of Holmes the public has seen over the years, and to work out whether her apparent sincerity is genuine or another performance.

## Part two: an actor stands in

The second half jumps to 2025. Holmes is by then serving her sentence in a minimum-security federal prison in Texas and is largely unavailable to the filmmakers. Fielder hires an actor to stand in for her and re-enacts their conversations with that actor.

Those scenes pull the film further from conventional documentary. Fielder and the actor go back over Holmes' behaviour and her possible motivations, and the staged exchanges blur the line between what was recorded and what was recreated. JournalArta's report does not name the actor.

Fielder also questions why Holmes let him into her private life at all. "I'm not the most, like, comforting," he says in the film.
