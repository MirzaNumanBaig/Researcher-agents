- The film has been in theatres since 6 November and is already drawing awards-season buzz, according to the Times.

## A filmmaker back in the spotlight

Martin McDonagh is making the interview rounds for Wild Horse Nine, his fifth feature. NPR's Fresh Air Weekend, published on 14 November, pairs a conversation with him with one with [Steve Buscemi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Buscemi), who NPR says appears in two new films, including Wild Horse Nine. Two weeks earlier, on 31 October, The New York Times Magazine published a lengthy Q&A with the director.

Both pieces return to a familiar question about McDonagh's work: why do such violent, sad stories feel so funny and so humane? The answers they offer are more hopeful than his reputation suggests.

## What the interviews reveal about Wild Horse Nine

According to NPR, the film centres on two CIA agents stationed in Chile in 1973. The New York Times adds that they are played by John Malkovich and [Sam Rockwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Rockwell), and that their mission is to destabilise the young democracy after the election of [Salvador Allende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Allende). The Times says the story pivots on a fateful trip to Easter Island.

NPR describes the film as a continuation of the ground McDonagh covered in [In Bruges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_Bruges) and The Banshees of Inisherin: violence, male friendship, and the weight of guilt and regret. The Times notes that the setting also lets him take on political power and what it calls America's foreign misadventures, alongside his usual crude humour and personal ethical dilemmas.

For more on the cast, see our earlier coverage of [Sam Rockwell on Wild Horse Nine and McDonagh's looser style](https://moduscarry.com/sam-rockwell-wild-horse-nine-mcdonagh/).

## Why America's image is part of the story

In the Times interview, McDonagh links the film to the current argument about America's place in the world. He says he is unsure what the country stands for right now. The film, he suggests, looks back at a period when America was widely seen in the West as the good guy, as children in the US, Britain and Ireland were taught.

He says that the more he read about what the CIA did in Chile, including helping to overthrow a democratically elected government that was followed by 15 years of fascist rule, the more he doubted that the West's moral story was the full one. These are his views as quoted by the Times, not findings from the film's makers as a group.

## "I'm so the opposite"

The most telling exchange comes when the interviewer points to a line in the film, "Life is sad, everything is sad", and a similar one from his play The Pillowman. McDonagh says that is not his view. In both cases, he explains, the line is the setup to a joke, spoken by a nasty, tough cop character.

He says his work comes out dark and sad in many places, but that he is the opposite in person. Growing up as a working-class kid in London, he says, he never expected to be in a position to make films at all.

That context matters for a director whose résumé is heavy with prizes. The Times puts the cumulative tally for his films at 18 Academy Award nominations, and notes that his short Six Shooter won the Oscar for best live-action short in 2004. It also describes him as an enfant terrible of British theatre before he moved to film.
