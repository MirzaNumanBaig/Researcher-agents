## Keanu Reeves Legend of Cinema award night

[Keanu Reeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keanu_Reeves) was honoured with the Legend of Cinema award on 30 October at the Lucas Theater, in an evening that combined a sold-out screening of The Matrix with a conversation about his craft. The event was covered by SCAD District, the student publication of the Savannah College of Art and Design.

The publication framed the award against a long career: the adrenaline of [Point Break](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_Break) (1991), the revenge saga of John Wick (2014) and the genre-changing world of The Matrix, which turned 27 this year.

## Why being present matters most

According to SCAD District, Reeves said the heart of acting is being present. A performance, he explained, only comes together when an actor is focused and fully immersed. What he enjoys most on set is the moment he stops feeling as though he is looking through his own eyes and starts seeing the world through his character's.

Small details count too. Reeves noted that some actors can plan a cough so well that even he believes it is real.

## The tomato sauce theory of acting

Asked how students should find a character, Reeves encouraged them to experiment. He compared the process to cooking. "This is your kitchen," he said. "We can all make a tomato sauce, but your tomato sauce is gonna be different than mine."

He added that actors should look for a character outside the script, from an accent to the way someone moves. "You have to make the clothes your own," he said. Once an actor finds their own groove, he suggested, the work stops feeling like a checklist and choices come more naturally.

## Takes, playbacks and a question for the students

On warming up and the number of takes, Reeves said it depends on the role and the director. Some directors want many takes, others only a few. He admitted there have been times he was unhappy with a performance and the director decided it was just right.

He prefers not to watch playbacks of his own scenes. He joked that younger actors often want to, then added that at 62 he considers anyone under 35 young.

One of the livelier moments came when Reeves questioned the students himself. He asked how their acting classes are structured, whether they read scenes from films or plays, and whether they can tell the difference. Students said they struggle with characters who are older or have lived through experiences they cannot relate to. Reeves urged them to find a personal way into those emotions.

The tone stayed light throughout. Asked about actors who grow difficult or refuse to leave their trailer, he said he had not met that in anyone he has worked with, then laughed that if it happened now, "there might be a knock on someone's door."
