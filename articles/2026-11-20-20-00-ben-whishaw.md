**Quick summary**

- London Theatre has reviewed *Eurotrash* at the Young Vic and calls [Ben Whishaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Whishaw) and [Kathryn Hunter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathryn_Hunter) "riveting" as a son and mother.
- The reviewer praises the dreamlike staging but says the play is a little too pleased with itself.
- The play runs at the Young Vic's Main House until 9 January 2027, with no interval and a running time of 1 hour 45 minutes.
- It is adapted by [Colin Teevan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Teevan) from Christian Kracht's novel and directed by Walter Meierjohann.

The first full review of *Eurotrash* is in, and it leans warm on the leads. According to [London Theatre](https://www.londontheatre.co.uk/reviews/eurotrash-review-young-vic), published on 20 November, Whishaw and Hunter are a striking pair in a Swiss Alps fantasy that works better as a family drama than as the kooky comedy it appears to be.

## What the play is about

The story follows Christian, played by Whishaw, who tries to make peace with his ageing mother, played by Hunter, by taking her on one final trip. London Theatre notes that the source novel is a work of autofiction, mixing memories of Kracht's Swiss childhood with a fantastical journey. In it, the son takes his mother from her hospital bed in Zurich and up into the mountains to see edelweiss.

The reviewer points out the joke at the centre of the trip: it is winter, and edelweiss blooms in warmer months. That gap, the review says, gives Walter Meierjohann's production its strange, dreamlike quality. ## How Whishaw and Hunter are rated

Hunter takes most of the praise. London Theatre describes her as impossible to look away from, cruelly impish and brisk, with a rasping voice and a ready supply of insults for her son. The review links her frailty act to her King Lear and singles out a stunt in which she skids across slippery ice after arriving on a zimmerframe.

Whishaw plays the other side of the pairing. The review calls his Christian sullen and disgruntled, an everyman whose adult rationality cannot override his hunger for his mother's approval. The two bond a little each time Christian, a novelist like the book's author, entertains her with stories. Visually, the reviewer notes, Hunter's fur coat and sunglasses sit against Whishaw's shabby wax jacket and scruffy ponytail.

## Where the review finds fault

The praise is not unqualified. London Theatre says the production slips when it becomes too fond of its own meta-theatricality, with a pause for applause when a waiter appears on demand and repeated nods to Christian writing his story in real time. Jokes about the mother's stoma bag are called overworked, and a giant splatter of excrement on stage tips the show into pantomime. The reviewer adds a wry "'tis the season".

A recurring theme of consumerism and grotesque wealth is also described as less well integrated. The mother has millions of Swiss francs, said to come from investing in the arms trade, and wants to give it away at random. Lizzie Clachan's set carries the idea with a conveyor belt of designer goods that drops handbags into a giant bin, but the review says it never quite joins up with the rest of the story.

Even so, the verdict ends on tenderness. The reviewer says there is plenty that is relatable in an adult son offering a peace offering to a hard-hearted mother, at a time of year when most people try to keep family tensions buried until after Boxing Day.

## Who else is in the production

Alongside the two leads, London Theatre credits Glyn Pritchard in several roles, including a taxi driver and a commune runner, and Deanna H. Choi, who reads announcements and plays violin from behind a glass window. The production is recommended for ages 12 and over.
